# Clock and time

The feeder needs the time to run meals on schedule, but it has no battery-backed clock. This page explains how Wellfed gets the time, keeps an honest bound on how wrong it might be, carries it across a restart, and behaves when it doesn't know it. The central idea: the clock never claims to know more than it does. Once its possible error passes 2 minutes it reads invalid, and the feeder says it needs the time.

Client syncs, the zone setting, NTP and the carry across restarts are observed on our unit. The clock needs a time source after each power-up. The long-uptime expiry and a learned rate have each been seen once on hardware ([Evidence](#evidence)); temperature and other units are unmeasured. The remaining physical checks are in [clock bench checks](clock-bench-checks.md).

Code: `wellfed-core/src/clock/` (`wall.rs`, `zone.rs`, `ntp.rs`), `wellfed-hal/src/clock.rs`, `wellfed-feeder/src/feeder/time.rs`, `wellfed-device/src/store/time.rs`, `wellfed-device-runtime/src/time.rs`, and `wellfed-client/src/time.rs` for the rule a client sends.

## Sources

- **A client's sync** (`Op::TimeSync`, inside a Noise session) carries the time, the zone's offset now and its next change. The app and Home Assistant send their host's clock and zone when the time view asks for it ([the cadence](#the-clock)), and `wellfed time` sends this computer's.
- **NTP** from the loop ([below](#ntp)), when the time settings name a server. Offline means no server and no cloud, not no network: the default server is the DHCP gateway, which many home routers answer for.

A fix is a time and how far off it could be. A client sync counts 1 s for its trip across the LAN. An NTP answer counts half its round trip less the server's processing, plus the server's root delay and dispersion, plus 3 ms of rounding.

## The clock

**What counts the time.** Between fixes the clock runs on the SysTimer, the 32 kHz free-running counter, corrected by the learned rate. It is extended to milliseconds at every clock access, and the loop makes one every pass.

- It does not use the scheduler's tick. Every flash lease masks interrupts and stops SysTick for its erase (about 45 ms), so the tick falls behind by each erase. The SysTimer runs through them, and the lease provider times its masked spans with it.
- A read that finds the count moved backwards, or by half its range (about 18 h without a read), forgets the time and clears the carry.
- The NTP exchange times its round trip on the same count, so an erase during an exchange widens the answer's error instead of shifting it.
- Records keep `at_ms` as uptime. `PLTIM1 sync` and `carry` add `count_ms`, the clock's count, whose lead over `at_ms` is the time the tick lost.

**When it is valid.** After a power-on, a brownout, or a reset that finds nothing to resume, the clock starts invalid. A warm reset (watchdog or software: a restart, an activation, a trial boot) resumes it ([warm resets](#warm-resets)).

**The trusted interval.** Only a trusted fix sets the clock's anchor: an owner's sync, or any fix while the clock is invalid (the first, or after expiry). From the anchor, the true time lies within the anchor's error plus the rate bound times the time since.

| Rate bound | Value | Gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unlearned (`UNLEARNED_PPM`) | 5,000 ppm | About 6.6 h of validity after a trusted fix |
| Learned floor (`LEARNED_FLOOR_PPM`) | 200 ppm | About 7 days. A learned correction is never trusted tighter |
| Bad fix (`RATE_LIMIT_PPM`) | ±10,000 ppm (1 %) | A measured rate past it is refused |

The SysTimer itself has measured 2 ppm on one NTP poll and 8 to 18 ppm over the 72-hour run, so both bounds are wide on purpose ([why](#evidence)).

**The cadence this gives:**

- **With NTP** (the gateway by default) nothing is needed. Polls every 17 minutes keep the reading, and at each expiry the next answer (within 64 s) re-sets it inside the old interval, so the clock reads invalid for up to a minute each time. No client syncs a valid clock while NTP is on: an owner's sync re-anchors it at the phone's time, and a phone a little off would then have each NTP answer miss the new interval, three misses would contest the clock, and the next sync would anchor it to the phone again.
- **Without NTP,** a client must sync it about every 6 h until an owner's syncs have taught the rate, then at least weekly. An owner's app keeps that cadence by itself while it reaches the feeder: `wellfed_client::time::check` asks an owner's client to sync a valid clock only with NTP off, once the reported error reaches half of `MAX_ERROR_MS` (`OWNER_SYNC_ERROR_MS`, 60 s). That is about 3.3 h after a fix while unlearned, halfway to expiry, and about 3.5 days once learned. The app checks on each read of the time in a session (every minute) and from Android's periodic catch-up (about every 15 minutes, far less in Doze).
- **An invalid clock** is synced by any client at once with NTP off. With NTP on it is NTP's to re-set first, so a client syncs it only once two of its reads at least 2 minutes apart (`INVALID_WAIT_MS`) both found it invalid in the same boot with no fix between them (the time view's sync count unchanged). The boot count alone can't say which boot: it lives in a backup register a power cut clears, so after one the count and the syncs can read as before. A client keeps each status read's uptime and when it read it, and counts as a new boot an uptime that went down, or more wall time between two reads than uptime, give or take a minute (`BOOT_MARGIN_MS`). A refusal is held only within the boot it came in. So a router that answers re-sets the clock inside its old interval, and one that never answers costs a few minutes of held meals at each expiry.
- **A time server that disagrees.** While the clock reads invalid and its server's last answer fell outside the interval it trusted (the time view's `ntp_last: bounded`), no client syncs it by itself (`ClockCheck::ServerDisagrees`). Either the server or the time the clock was last set to is wrong, and a client's sync, trusted while the clock is invalid, could be the wrong side: a phone 30 s off that anchored the clock would re-anchor it after each contest and start the next. Left alone, the clock takes the server's answer only once the forgotten rate's bound has grown to reach it: about 200 s for each second between them, so roughly an hour or two for a phone 30 s off, but about 8 days for a server an hour off. So the Clock screen says the time server disagrees and gives the remedies: an owner checks or changes the time source, or sets the time from a phone whose time is right (**Set the time from this phone**); a member is told to ask an owner. Home Assistant raises a repair saying the same. Nothing in the device API changes for it: the time view's `ntp_last` already says so. No session sends a sync for pairing either: the sync goes, or not, on the first time read.
- **A client that disagrees with the feeder** sends nothing by itself. When its clock and the feeder's differ by more than the feeder's error plus 2 s (`AGREE_MARGIN_MS`), one of them is wrong, and an automatic sync would either fight NTP or move the feeder to a wrong phone's time. The app's Clock screen says "This phone and the feeder disagree about the time" with the gap, and an owner can set the time from the phone by hand there, after checking the phone's.
- **Spacing.** Each client keeps a memo per feeder (`wellfed_client::time::AutoSync`): when it last sent an automatic sync, when its current run of invalid reads began, and the view a sync was refused on. The app keeps it in `alerts.json` beside the feeder's watch, so sessions and catch-ups share it and it survives the process: one automatic sync per feeder at most every 30 minutes, phone-wide, landed or not. Home Assistant keeps it for the session thread's life and spaces its syncs 5 minutes apart, so a meal the clock held still runs within the [missed-meal rule](#missed-meal-rule)'s 15 minutes. After `Refusal::Time` a client sends no automatic sync until the clock's view changes (its validity, sync count or last source). A sync the owner asks for by hand goes outside the memo.
- **A member's or Home Assistant's client** syncs only a clock that reads invalid, since its sync can only narrow a valid one. Home Assistant sends its own time zone (`hass.config.time_zone`), not the host's, which in a container is UTC, and reads the time view again at each status that finds the clock invalid, so a change of time source is in the view its rule runs on.
- **An offset.** A feeder with no zone and no offset (a power cut cleared the one a sync gave) holds its meals. A session gives it one with a sync, but an owner's only with no time server, since it re-anchors the clock; with one, Today offers the owner this phone's zone instead.
- So a home without NTP stays set while an owner's app reaches the feeder every few hours. Otherwise it can set a public pool's address (`wellfed time --ntp IP[:PORT]`; hostnames are not resolved yet), or choose one on the app's Clock screen.

**Drift learning** comes from owners' syncs only:

- An owner's sync is measured against a baseline: the first owner's sync since the rate was last forgotten. Once it measures the rate to within 50 ppm (`LEARN_MAX_PPM`), the correction applies and the bound drops to the floor.
- Only syncs from the baseline's client key measure the rate. Two owners' phones whose clocks differ by a second would otherwise teach the feeder their difference over the span as drift. Another owner key's sync still re-anchors the clock, but it neither teaches the rate nor replaces the baseline, so a second phone checking in can't keep restarting the first one's measurement. The baseline changes hands only when the rate is forgotten (a restart, an owner's sync that disagrees with the clock, three NTP misses) or once it is half the uptime count's range old (about 24.8 days). The key is the session's, held in RAM with the baseline; nothing new is stored or sent. Removing the baseline's key from the feeder doesn't clear it: until one of those, no other owner's syncs teach the rate, though they keep the clock set. Restarting the feeder after removing an owner clears it.
- A later measurement that disagrees widens the bound by the difference. An owner's sync that disagrees with the clock forgets the learned rate.
- The rate is not stored or carried across a reset, so each boot learns it again.
- With owner syncs of NTP-like error a few tens of minutes apart, the rate is learned within the window. Owner syncs alone (1 s of error each) need about 11 hours, so the second lands after the first window expired and is trusted then.
- NTP alone never teaches the rate, so an NTP-only clock stays at the unlearned bound; its polls make that no worse than a learned one. A first owner's sync then becomes the baseline, and a second measures the rate.
- A free NTP set (the first fix after a power-on, or one re-setting an expired or contested clock) is never a baseline. Otherwise an attacker's boot-time answer 100 s off would let an owner's sync six hours later learn +4,630 ppm with a 200 ppm bound, leaving the clock 333 s off at 20 h while it claimed about 15 s.

**Why NTP fixes teach no rate.** An NTP fix only had to fall inside the trusted interval (or, re-setting an expired clock, the expired one), so it can sit anywhere in it. A rate measured from two of them is uncertain by the interval's width over the span: the unlearned bound again. It could move the reading but never tighten the bound, which is all learning is for. Learning from them would let an answerer at the edge each expiry teach a skewed rate, and walk the clock about 30 minutes in three days while it claimed the learned bound.

**Untrusted fixes** (NTP, or a member's or the server's sync) must fall inside the trusted interval, or they are refused: `Refusal::Time`, `PLTIM1 sync ok=0 error=bounded`, or NTP's `bounded`. Inside, the reading moves to the overlap of the two intervals. Its reported error never drops below what the anchor allows: the reading's distance from the anchor's estimate plus the anchor's error. So an untrusted fix can improve where the clock reads, but cannot make it claim more than it knows, teach it a rate, or walk it outside the trusted interval. Everything is kept in microseconds internally, so rounding cannot walk it either.

**Validity and expiry.** The clock reads invalid once its reported error passes 2 minutes (`MAX_ERROR_MS`), sooner than the table says if untrusted fixes moved the reading, which the error counts. Then:

- An owner's or a client's sync sets it freely, and the NTP exchange asks within 64 s instead of waiting out its poll.
- An NTP answer must fall inside the expired anchor's interval, carried to now and grown at the rate bound with no two-minute cap (about ±2 minutes just after expiry). Outside, it is refused `bounded` and counts as a miss. So an answerer at the edge every expiry walks the clock about 2 minutes per 6.6 h.
- Three NTP answers in a row that miss the trusted interval mean the anchor, the rate or the server is wrong. The clock reads invalid rather than staying silently valid, and needs the time. It keeps the anchor as NTP's bound all the same, as after expiry: an owner's or a client's sync sets it freely, and an NTP answer only inside the old interval. NTP is unauthenticated, so the next answer can't be the one to decide which side is wrong. The clock also forgets its learned rate, which may be what is wrong: the bound then grows at 5,000 ppm, so an honest answer re-sets the clock once the bound reaches it, with no client's sync. A contested clock left about 24.8 days with no fix (half the uptime count's range) loses its bound, and the next answer then sets any time, as after a power-on. A member's disagreement does not count toward the three.

**What this bounds.** An attacker answering NTP hourly at the edge of the interval never makes a valid reading claim less error than it has. With an owner's sync every 12 hours the clock stays honest for a week; without one it expires (within 7 h) rather than lie. One spoofed answer teaches no rate. What NTP is trusted with is setting an invalid clock within its old interval, and a clock with no anchor (the first fix after a power-on) to anything. So an on-path attacker can walk the clock about 2 minutes each expiry, or make it read invalid with three misses, which holds the plans until a sync or an answer inside the old interval. A warm reset carries the old interval too. It can't step an anchored clock a day ahead and run tomorrow's meals. An owner's sync, or the app showing the time, is the check. A feeding plan that must not run on an unconfirmed time can treat as unconfirmed a clock whose last fix was NTP after a `First`, with no owner sync since (the time view's `last: Ntp`).

**Steps and jumps.** A fix that moves the clock by at most 2 s is a step. Anything else is a jump, logged with its signed size and recorded as a `ClockSync` event.

**Refused fixes** leave the clock unchanged: a time before 2026 or after 2099, or, for a sync, an offset outside −12 h to +14 h or a next change that is not in the future.

## Time without a source

With no valid clock the feeder needs the time, and the device API says so: `Status.clock_ms` is `None`, and so are the time view's `clock_ms` and `error_ms`. Scheduled meals hold by the [missed-meal rule](#missed-meal-rule). The Dispense button and client feeds still work, since no feed path reads the wall clock. Showing the missing time on the panel is light policy.

## Time zone

The zone is a feeder setting (`PutTime`'s `zone`): a POSIX TZ rule such as `MST7MDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0`. The feeder evaluates it for any year without a zone database (`wellfed_core::clock::zone`).

The evaluator reads each year's two changes alone, and a meal's local minute is placed by looking a day either side of it, so the parser refuses a rule with a change within a day of New Year or two changes less than two days apart. No tzdb zone comes near either; only a hand-written rule could. A rule that keeps daylight time from New Year to New Year (`EST5EDT,0/0,J365/25`, zic's way of writing permanent daylight time) reads as the one offset it is.

A client turns the owner's IANA name into the rule with `wellfed_client::time::rule`:

- It takes the footer of the zone's TZif file (RFC 8536) from the tzdb that `jiff-tzdb` bundles.
- It checks the footer with the feeder's own parser (offsets −12 h to +14 h, as syncs). It also checks it against both the bundled zone and the host's view of it, which the caller passes in, on both sides of every transition of the next ten years, the rule's own included. So a rule that keeps daylight time a newer host tzdb has dropped is refused (`RuleError::HostDisagrees`), even in winter, when the two still agree.
- A footer only describes the zone after its file's last listed transition. So a zone that lists irregular changes ahead is refused (`RuleError::Irregular`) and stays on the syncs' offsets. No zone in the current tzdb is (host-tested over every zone).
- Every footer fits the 48 bytes the store keeps.

Without a zone setting, local time comes from the last client sync's offset and next change, which is correct across one change. A rule wins over a sync's offsets. The app's Clock screen sets it to the phone's zone, or to any zone the owner searches for in the bundled tzdb's names (`wellfed_client::time::zones`, each area and place and `UTC`), and clears it. `wellfed time --zone America/Denver` (or `--zone system`) sets it, and `--clear-zone` clears it.

## NTP

`wellfed_core::clock::ntp` is the exchange, sans-IO. `wellfed_feeder::feeder::time` runs it in the loop task on one non-blocking UDP socket, as the mDNS responder does, so it needs no task of its own.

- **Server.** The time settings' `ntp`: `Off`, `Gateway { port }` (the default: the lease's gateway, port 123) or `Address { ip, port }`. The default trusts nothing about the gateway except that it is on the path: an unauthenticated answer comes from whoever holds the gateway's address and sees the request's nonce. That bounds what it can do to a valid clock (above), not what it can set an invalid one to. Hostnames (`pool.ntp.org`) would need a DNS lookup in the loop, and are not supported yet. Nothing is sent before DHCP gives an address, and a changed server or gateway is asked at once.
- **Requests** carry nothing but a random 64-bit nonce in the transmit timestamp (RFC 9109's data minimization). An answer must come from the server's address and port and echo the nonce, checked first, so a datagram of any other shape leaves the exchange waiting for the real answer. It must then be a version 3 or 4 server answer, synchronized (leap not 3, stratum 1–15), dated 2026–2099 (NTP era 1 from 2036 is read), and loose by at most 10 s.
- **Pacing.** One request at a time, with an answer due within 2 s. A lost or unusable answer retries after 8 s, backing off to 64 s. After a good one, the next goes 1024 s (about 17 minutes) later, or within 64 s while the clock needs the time. An answer the clock refuses as outside its interval while it needs the time (expired or contested) holds the next request for 8 s, doubling with each such answer to 1024 s, and an answer it uses resets that; without the hold, the clock's need would ask again on every pass, for as long as the server and the clock disagree. A kiss-o'-death `RATE` holds the exchange for an hour, and `DENY` or `RSTR` for a day. Nothing shortens a hold, not even a clock that needs the time.
- **Records.** `PLAPI1 ntp server=… | socket open=… | answer result=used|bounded|refused error_ms=… | refused reason=… | lost`. The clock logs `PLTIM1 sync ok=1 source=ntp kind=first|step|jump delta_ms=… error_ms=… at_ms=…`. A first fix and a jump are recorded in the history as `ClockSync` by `Source::Feeder`. A step is not, so polling does not wear the event ring.

## Time settings

`GetTime` and `PutTime { base, changes }` are ops of the [device API](device-api.md)'s shared core, answered `Time(TimeView)`.

- `GetTime` is anyone's.
- `PutTime` is an owner's only, since the NTP server decides which fixes the clock hears. It is answered `Done` once the store holds the settings asked for (or already held them), and refused `NotWritable` when the writer could not write them.
- The view carries the settings' generation, whether they are writable, the zone's rule, the server, the clock (`clock_ms`, `error_ms`, `offset_s`), the learned rate and its bound, the sync count, the last fix's source and age, and the last NTP result.

**Storage.** The settings live in their own store, storage sectors 27 and 28 (`wellfed_device::store::time`): a 128-byte dual-slot record, magic `PLFTIM01`, version 1, pinned in `store_layout.rs`. The settings writer opens it with the Wi-Fi store and writes it, as every store write, under the write turn and the arming attempt marker. With no record the defaults apply: no zone, NTP from the gateway on port 123. The device's settings record (sectors 2 and 3) carried an unused `utc_offset_minutes` field until its major 5 dropped it. A build that predates this store never touches sectors 27 and 28, so a bank 0 running one keeps its own settings. The [factory reset](pairing.md#factory-reset) erases them with the other settings.

## Warm resets

The clock survives a warm reset, but not a power cut. The KM0 RTC counts seconds across the reset, and 64 bytes of KM4 RAM hold the last known time.

**The RTC.** The KM0 RTC (`0x48004000`) is an always-on BCD calendar on the 32 kHz clock: a 9-bit day count, and hours, minutes and seconds. It counts seconds from power-on although nothing initializes it (`ISR`'s INITS flag is clear at every boot). It keeps counting across watchdog, activation and trial resets, and restarts at 0 on a power cut. The firmware never writes it, and logs it read-only at boot (`PLTIM1 rtc tr=… cr=… isr=… prer=… at_ms=…`).

**Where the time waits.** It needs somewhere a warm reset keeps and a power cut loses (recovered from the pinned SDK):

- **KM0 backup registers** survive warm resets, but every register the SDK leaves to the user is taken: 1 the attempt and update bits and the lease fault, 2 the silent-reset breadcrumb, 3 KM4's boot count, 4 the peer boot count and the trial count, 5 the KM0 heartbeat.
- **Retention SRAM** (1 KiB at `0x000C0000`) is zeroed by KM0's start code on every boot that is not a deep-sleep wake (`rtl8721dlp_app_start.c`).
- **KM4's main RAM** is not cleared by a warm reset in any code the SDK ships:
  - The KM4 boot loader (`boot_flash_hp.c`) clears its own `.bss` in its secure region, and copies only the image's RAM data (`.ram_image2.entry`, `.text`, `.data`, the sections the image's `ram_2.bin` holds).
  - `app_start` clears only `.ram_image2.bss`.
  - The FreeRTOS heap (`heap_5`, `CONFIG_DYNAMIC_HEAP_SIZE`) runs from `__bfsram_end__` to `__sram_end__`, the top of `BD_RAM_NS` at `0x1007C000`. The ROM's and boot loader's RAM, the secure stack and the non-secure main stack lie outside it (`0x1007C000` and up, below `0x10005000`).

So the builder's KM4 linker script takes the top 64 bytes from the heap (`SdkAnchor::SramEnd`) and names them `wellfed_time_carry`, at `0x1007BFC0`. The address is the same in every build, so a record survives an activation into a new build. The 880 bytes below hold the [call trail](startup-diagnostics.md#the-call-trail), so the heap ends at `0x1007BC50`. `example_entry.c` loads and stores the 16 words and cleans the store out of the D-cache. The SDK's header says the cache writes through; the clean holds either way.

**The record** (`WallClock::carry`, in RAM only, so no flash format) holds the RTC second, the trusted estimate, its error and the reading at that second (in microseconds), the zone hint, the last fix's origin and age, a version and a CRC-32. Keeping the last fix's origin keeps the time view's `last`, and with it whether an owner confirmed the time. The record is written after every accepted fix and every 10 s from the loop, and cleared while the clock is invalid, so a reset never finds a clock older than the last correction, or one that had expired. A contested clock (three NTP misses) is the exception: its record carries its anchor and says it is contested, since the anchor is all that bounds the next NTP answer. The record's version is 2 (`CARRY_VERSION`); any other version is refused as `corrupt`.

**Resuming** happens in `WallClock::resume`, from the start hook, before anything reads the clock. It logs `PLTIM1 carry result=resumed gap_s=… error_ms=… rtc_s=… at_ms=…` (`result=contested` for a contested clock, which resumes still reading invalid, with no two-minute limit on its error), or `result=cold|empty|corrupt|rtc|backwards|stale|expired|set`. A record resumes only when all of these hold:

- the boot follows a watchdog or software reset, by the ROM's reset flags (a power-on, brownout or deep-sleep wake is `cold`);
- the record passes its magic, version, CRC and range checks;
- the RTC reads as a 24-hour count, with two reads that agree;
- the RTC is no earlier than the record's second (`backwards`: the RTC restarted), and at most 600 s after it (`stale`).

A gap of `g` RTC seconds moves both estimates by `g` s. It grows the error by 1 s (both ends are whole RTC seconds) plus `(g + 1)` × 10,000 ppm (`RTC_PPM`), wide since the gap is short. A grown error past 2 minutes is `expired`. The resumed interval is trusted, as the saved one was, since it only grew from a trusted fix; untrusted fixes still only narrow it. It is no baseline for a rate: the rate and sync count start again, while the last fix keeps its origin and age. Either way, the boot then stores its own record or clears it.

**What it does not catch.** Every build that resumes clears the record at a cold boot, but a build from before the carry leaves it alone and uses that RAM as heap top. So a record could outlive a power cut if all of these held: the RAM kept those 64 bytes through the power loss, the next boot ran such a build, and a later warm reset into a resuming build found the RTC within 600 s past the record's second. The gap would then miss the time before the cut. It needs bank 0 to hold such a build, and goes away once bank 0 runs one that resumes. A bank 0 build that counts on the tick also leaves the trial boot unsynced after a hop through it ([tick loss](#evidence)).

## Missed-meal rule

`wellfed_core::clock::wall::occurrence(due, evaluated, now)` decides each scheduled occurrence. `evaluated` is a persisted mark per plan: the latest occurrence already run, missed or before the plan's save ([schedules](schedules.md#at-most-once)).

| Situation | Result |
| --- | --- |
| No valid clock | Nothing runs |
| `due` after `now` | Later |
| `due` at or before the mark | Evaluated: never runs twice, even after a backward jump |
| `due` in (mark, now], at most 15 min late | Runs once |
| `due` in (mark, now], more than 15 min late | Missed: reported, never run |

So a forward jump, a restart or a long unsynced spell runs only occurrences at most 15 minutes late, and a backward jump never repeats a meal.

## Evidence

- **Expiry after a long uptime.** Observed (November 1, 2026, hopper empty, NTP lost, about 15 hours up): the clock expired about 6.6 hours after its last sync. A 09:30 meal then held, with no reminder and no feed. A manual feed still ran. A sync at 09:32:50 ran the held meal late, once.
- **The bound across an install.** Observed (November 1, 2026): after 14 hours of owner syncs the clock had learned its rate, and its error grew at the 1,500 ppm floor rather than 5,000. The carry read `error_ms=5204` on the first restart of an install, 35 minutes after a sync, and 6638 on the second, as the bound predicts. The rate is not carried, so the error then grew at 5,000 ppm again.
- **Client syncs and jumps.** Observed (October 22): the first sync from the server (`PLTIM1 sync ok=1 kind=first`, MDT with the November 1 change) put health's clock within 0.2 s of the server, with an uncertainty of 1.06 s. With `examples/time_jump.rs` (since removed), a +1 h and a −2 h jump were classified (`jump_ms` 3600097 and −7200007). Two clean 10-minute resyncs found +442 and +439 ms: the tick-counted clock ran about 735 ppm slow.
- **Zone and first NTP fix.** Observed (October 26, v232, `combo-fw46-20261026-01`…`04`): the zone setting survived a reboot and a power cycle. A first NTP fix had an error of 25 ms and recorded a `ClockSync`. The next poll, 1,026 s later, was refused `bounded`: the tick-counted clock ran about 2,060 ppm slow against the host's timesyncd clock, past the then 2,000 ppm bound.
- **The RTC.** Observed (October 26, v232): it kept counting across watchdog, activation and trial resets (1,613 s against the host's 1,613.1 s) and restarted at 0 on a power cut.
- **Tick loss.** Observed, with the cause recovered from the logs' arithmetic (October 26). The carry held across `wellfed reboot` (`gap_s=2 error_ms=2131`, within 0.3 s of the host) and read `cold` after a power cut. After a v237 install through bank 0's recovery role, both boots logged `resumed` (bank 0 `gap_s=6 error_ms=3852 rtc_s=438`, the trial `gap_s=6 error_ms=5174 rtc_s=506`), yet the clock read 12.2 s behind the host while claiming 5,510 ms. Bank 0 ran 62 RTC seconds before its last store, but its error grew only about 252 ms at 5,000 ppm (5,174 − 3,852 − the trial's 1,070 ms of RTC allowance): its tick saw about 50 s, losing about 12 s across the bank's 249 erases. Modeled: on the tick, the same download in `wellfed-hal`'s `clock.rs` leaves the clock 11.2 s off while claiming 1.3 s, hence the SysTimer.
